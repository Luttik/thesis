## Participant Profile

Andreea Bulisache has spent ~20 years in technology spanning Telco, IT, cybersecurity, and Microsoft. At Microsoft, she worked in the acquisition and strategy team, was early in OpenAI conversations, and dealt with AI ethics, skill, and bias considerations. For the past three years she has run an independent advisory practice focused on AI governance for boards and executives. She helps boards understand AI before they adopt it — running war room sessions, scenario planning, vendor procurement criteria design, and governance framework development. Her scope extends beyond technology into geopolitics, capacity planning, energy considerations, and regulatory compliance (EU AI Act, data sovereignty). She describes her "superpower" as simplifying complex technology for decision-makers who are empowered to make decisions but lack deep understanding. She is not a marketing specialist and explicitly states her limited understanding of marketing, making her perspective valuable as a domain-agnostic governance lens applied to marketing questions.

## 1. AI Applications & Use Cases (Conceptual/Advisory)

Andreea does not implement marketing AI herself. Her use cases are frameworks she advises on. They represent what she sees as the value levers, not implemented systems.

### 1.1 Decision Velocity
**Code:** `AI-APPLICATION:DECISION-VELOCITY`

AI accelerating the speed of marketing decisions — not creating content faster (generative) but making strategic decisions faster through campaign testing loops, pricing experimentation, and segmentation refinement.

> "One would be the decision velocity [...] they would be able to make faster decisions [...] Campaign testing Loops [...] pricing experimentation. Segmentation refinement, so that would be supporting the velocity. And the decision velocity not creating velocity." (line 95)

### 1.2 Precision at Scale
**Code:** `AI-APPLICATION:PRECISION-AT-SCALE`

Moving from segment-based marketing to individual-level orchestration. AI agents trained to react faster and anticipate individual needs, with autonomous A/B experimentation and continuous learning loops.

> "Precision at scale, whether it's segment based or periodic optimization [...] they would be able to increase the granularity to each individual, so they would know exactly how to profile Andrea based on everything." (line 101)

> "Autonomous a b experimentation [...] continuous learning Loops, so I think from every little thing if you know exactly what you're tracking that would feed back and train your agent." (line 107)

### 1.3 Workflow Reinvention
**Code:** `AI-APPLICATION:WORKFLOW-REINVENTION`

Not just adopting AI tools but redesigning how work flows between agents and humans. Splitting activities, establishing oversight (managing operations) and foresight (anticipating trends). The distinction: "autonomous agent runs lifecycle experimentation" rather than "AI helps me write emails."

> "Reinvention of workflows [...] optimal redesigning or delegate execution to agents, so there will be the split of activities between the humans and different agents [...] oversight and foresight." (line 110)

### 1.4 Marketing Intelligence
**Code:** `AI-APPLICATION:MARKETING-INTELLIGENCE`

Monitoring competitors on a live sentiment scale, detecting pricing signals, and generating strategic foresight for product iteration.

> "Marketing intelligence [...] monitoring competitors. On a life sentimental skill, detect pricing signals [...] strategic foresight plus their product iteration." (lines 113–119)

## 2. Five Dimensions of Agentic AI (Framework)

**Code:** `CONCEPT:FIVE-DIMENSIONS-AGENTIC`

Andreea defines agentic AI through five dimensions, each with perceived and implied value:

1. **Planning** — most understood
2. **Acting autonomously** — once the pattern is clarified
3. **Executing workflows** — "a huge problem, because a lot of these workflows are not explicit"
4. **Coordinating tools** — future evolution, implies orchestration
5. **Learning from outcomes** — integrating outcomes back into how agents run

> "I see it as in having five Dimensions. One is the planning component [...] acting autonomously [...] executing workflows, which is a huge problem [...] coordinating tools [...] learn from outcomes." (lines 83–86)

## 3. Benefits

### 3.1 Decision Velocity
**Code:** `BENEFIT:DECISION-VELOCITY`

Faster, better-informed marketing decisions. Not just faster content creation but accelerated strategic decision-making on campaigns, pricing, and segmentation.

### 3.2 Precision at Scale
**Code:** `BENEFIT:PRECISION-AT-SCALE`

Lower cost of acquisition through individual-level targeting, different ROI measurement, and continuous optimization loops.

### 3.3 Clarity of Direction
**Code:** `BENEFIT:CLARITY-OF-DIRECTION`

For mature companies, the process materializes into an evolutionary road map with granular projects indicating data governance needs, analyst requirements, and technology decisions. The benefit is clarity itself.

> "This would probably materialize into a road map, an evolutionary road map [...] it does provide that the clarity of Direction. If you don't have, it's hard to act." (lines 350–353)

Boards and executives feel equipped to make justified decisions. They can defend their choices to auditors and shareholders because they understand what they decided and why.

Beyond education: redesigning org structure, rethinking human-agent coexistence, and reshaping how customers are served. The Ripple effects span from data governance to hiring.

## 4. Drawbacks & Risks

### 4.1 Over-Deployment of Technology
**Code:** `RISK:OVER-DEPLOYMENT`

More technology does not always mean more value. Poorly integrated systems create inefficiency rather than enabling it. Complicated systems with skewed inputs limit outputs.

> "Over deployment of Technology leads to inefficiencies, not always enables inefficient. So there is such thing as having too much technology if it's not well integrated." (line 365)

### 4.2 Vendor Dependency / Default Strategy
**Code:** `RISK:VENDOR-DEPENDENCY`

AI strategy shaped by the default vendor (Microsoft, IBM) rather than actual business needs. The vendor's input becomes the strategy, which may not reflect the organization's desired outcome.

> "Most of the thinking behind an AI strategy comes from their default vendor, so it's Microsoft AI strategy or IBM [...] To which extent this reflects your outcome?" (lines 251–254)

### 4.3 Rush to Execution
**Code:** `RISK:RUSH-TO-EXECUTION`

Executives want to jump straight to tools and execution without going through clarity, awareness, and readiness stages. This creates exposure and surfaces problems that should have been anticipated.

> "There are a lot of instances. Executives want to get into execution [...] if you don't go through all of these faces. Chances are, you might not anticipate what are the risk and synergies." (line 359)

### 4.4 Treating AI as Technology (Not Systemic)
**Code:** `RISK:TECHNOLOGY-LENS`

If AI is treated as a technology problem, its impact is measured in licenses. If treated as systemic, it triggers rethinking of go-to-market, org structure, and cross-functional collaboration. Most organizations default to the technology lens.

> "If you treat AI as a technology [...] the risk is that you're gonna measure its impact in licenses. If you treat it based on systemic impact, then you start revisiting. How is it that you go to market." (line 338)

## 5. Organizational Enablers

### 5.1 CARE Framework
**Code:** `ENABLER:CARE-FRAMEWORK`

Andreea's personal model: Clarity → Awareness → Readiness → Execution. Skipping stages (especially jumping to execution) creates exposure. Each stage builds on the previous.

> "Clarity, awareness, Readiness, and execution [...] if you don't have Clarity, jumping into execution will only surface a lot of problems." (line 359)

### 5.2 Outcome vs. Output Thinking
**Code:** `ENABLER:OUTCOME-THINKING`

Starting from the desired outcome (business goal) rather than optimizing current outputs (what AI produces from current data). Output = what you get from inputs. Outcome = what you actually want to achieve. The gap between them determines what additional data sources, processes, or changes are needed.

> "Based on your existing data sources [...] it generates an output [...] if you start with an outcome, which was your desired outcome. You might have to alter the output of your data with additional sources." (line 242)

### 5.3 Systems Thinking
**Code:** `ENABLER:SYSTEMS-THINKING`

Understanding the whole ecosystem — a system of systems — not just individual tools or departments. Forces convergence between CTO, CFO, and CRO because AI's impact crosses all their domains.

> "System thinking and then a system of systems. So, I understand the whole ecosystem." (line 224)

> "You force collaboration between the CTO, the CFO, the chief risk officer. Because if you solve for this in isolation, then it's not gonna work." (line 338)

### 5.4 Education Before Adoption
**Code:** `ENABLER:EDUCATION-FIRST`

War room sessions that expose executives to scenarios before any tool decisions. Includes geopolitical risk, supply chain dependencies, energy costs, and regulatory implications. The goal is to make them comfortable enough with AI to make informed decisions.

> "My engagement typically starts with an AI governance war room [...] sessions that exposes them to different scenarios in which they are confronted with the way they take decisions." (line 275)

### 5.5 Definition of Good
**Code:** `ENABLER:DEFINITION-OF-GOOD`

Explicitly defining what "good" looks like from both an AI standpoint and a company-wide perspective, including customer and stakeholder perception. Without this, there's no basis for evaluation.

> "What does good look like from an AI standpoint? What does a good look like from a company overall perspective? The perceived impact in the eyes of their customers and their stakeholders." (line 284)

### 5.6 Decoupling Art from Science
**Code:** `ENABLER:ART-SCIENCE-DECOUPLING`

Separating what can be automated (the "science" — repeatable, pattern-based) from what requires human judgment (the "art" — creative, contextual). Technology should be applied to the science; humans retained for the art.

> "There's a component that is science. Which can be automated, and there's a component that is art, which is the creative [...] you need to decouple the art from the science and use technology for what it's worth." (line 176)

## 6. Organizational Obstacles

### 6.1 Workflows Not Explicit
**Code:** `OBSTACLE:IMPLICIT-WORKFLOWS`

Processes are not documented or standardized, making AI/agent adoption impossible. This is identified as "a huge problem" for executing the agentic workflow dimension.

> "Executing workflows, which is a huge problem, because a lot of these workflows are not explicit." (line 83)

### 6.2 Marketing Lacks Process Orientation
**Code:** `OBSTACLE:NO-PROCESS-CULTURE`

Marketing professionals find comfort in bespoke engagements and resist standardization. They perceive their work as "complicated" (which gives them value) rather than "complex" (which could be mapped and partially automated).

> "There's some personality profiles that probably reside more in marketing that find value perceived value in sitting on a complicated process [...] whereas for someone from external, I think there is a component that is science." (line 176)

### 6.3 Automation vs. Augmentation Gap
**Code:** `OBSTACLE:AUTOMATION-VS-AUGMENTATION`

It is easier to automate clear, well-defined processes than to augment ambiguous, knowledge-intensive ones. Augmentation requires understanding what data provides what insight — a step most organizations haven't taken.

> "It's easier to think about automation if things are clear [...] I think it's harder to augment [...] limited available data or sense of system logic." (lines 131–134)

### 6.4 Executive Comfort ≠ Understanding
**Code:** `OBSTACLE:COMFORT-NOT-UNDERSTANDING`

Executives want to discuss AI and feel comfortable conversing about it, but their comfort does not reflect genuine understanding. They arrive wanting "an Innovation session" and leave confronted with how short-sighted they are.

> "The Comfort that boards have [...] does not necessarily reflect their understanding. So very often, you would find Executives want to converse about AI [...] I never sold for what they want to be solved before I bring them back into understanding." (lines 305–311)

### 6.5 Solution Before Problem
**Code:** `OBSTACLE:SOLUTION-BEFORE-PROBLEM`

Executives want a product or application, then look for a problem to solve with it. The approach should be reversed: identify the problem, then determine whether to buy, build, or acquire.

> "They want an application or a product for which they need to find out what could potentially be the problem to solve [...] you first need to see, what is it that you're trying to solve." (line 323)

## 7. Conceptual Codes

### 7.1 Outcome vs. Output
**Code:** `CONCEPT:OUTCOME-VS-OUTPUT`

The critical distinction: output is what AI produces from current data and prompts. Outcome is the desired business end state. The gap between them is where the real work (and value) lies. Most organizations optimize outputs; few define outcomes.

> "If you start with an outcome [...] You might have to alter the output of your data with additional sources of data. [...] Input output? You're not coming [to the outcome]." (lines 242–245)

### 7.2 CARE Model
**Code:** `CONCEPT:CARE-MODEL`

Clarity → Awareness → Readiness → Execution. A maturity model for AI adoption where each stage is prerequisite for the next. Most organizations try to start at Execution.

### 7.3 Automation vs. Augmentation
**Code:** `CONCEPT:AUTOMATION-VS-AUGMENTATION`

Two distinct modes of AI application. Automation replaces well-defined processes. Augmentation enhances ambiguous, judgment-intensive work. The agentic AI promise is augmentation, but adoption is stuck at automation because augmentation requires deeper organizational understanding.

### 7.4 Systemic vs. Technology Lens
**Code:** `CONCEPT:SYSTEMIC-VS-TECHNOLOGY`

If AI is viewed as technology → measured in licenses, adopted in silos. If viewed systemically → triggers business model rethinking, cross-functional convergence, and organizational redesign. The lens determines the ceiling of value.

> "If you treat AI as a technology [...] the risk is that you're gonna measure its impact in licenses. If you treat it based on systemic impact, then you start revisiting." (line 338)

### 7.5 Art vs. Science in Marketing
**Code:** `CONCEPT:ART-VS-SCIENCE`

Marketing contains both repeatable, pattern-based elements (science) and creative, judgment-intensive elements (art). AI should be applied to the science; humans retained for the art. The failure mode is applying AI to both indiscriminately.

### 7.6 Perceived Value vs. Implied Value
**Code:** `CONCEPT:PERCEIVED-VS-IMPLIED-VALUE`

For each of the five agentic AI dimensions, there is a perceived value (what people think it's worth) and an implied value (what it actually delivers). The gap between these drives both disappointment and missed opportunities.

### 7.7 Over-Deployment as Anti-Pattern
**Code:** `CONCEPT:OVER-DEPLOYMENT`

More technology ≠ more value. Without integration and architecture, additional technology creates complexity that limits outputs. A perspective that "someone who does not understand technology would not understand."

## Key Takeaways for Thesis

1. **Outcome vs. output is the critical distinction:** Most organizations optimize AI outputs (what comes out of current data + prompts). Few define outcomes (what they actually want to achieve). The gap between these determines how much value AI creates. This reframes the entire thesis question from "how do marketing managers create value" to "do they even define what value means before they start?"
2. **CARE framework provides a maturity model:** Clarity → Awareness → Readiness → Execution maps directly onto the dynamic capabilities framework (sensing → seizing → transforming) and provides a practical adoption sequence that most organizations skip.
3. **Automation vs. augmentation explains the adoption gap:** The reason agentic AI is underadopted in marketing (beyond analytics) is that augmentation requires understanding processes that aren't documented. Organizations default to automation because it's easier, even though augmentation is where the higher value lies.
4. **The systemic lens determines the value ceiling:** Treating AI as a technology problem caps value at efficiency. Treating it systemically enables business transformation. This echoes Rolf's effectiveness-vs-efficiency argument from a governance perspective.
5. **Executive comfort ≠ understanding:** Boards want to talk about AI but don't understand it well enough to make informed decisions. Education before adoption is the single most important enabler from Andreea's perspective.
6. **Marketing's "complicated vs. complex" identity:** Marketers find value in perceived complexity, resisting standardization. From an external perspective, much of what they do is complex (mappable, partially automatable), not complicated (requiring bespoke handling every time).
7. **First pure governance perspective:** Andreea is the first participant who focuses exclusively on how organizations should approach AI, not on what they should build with it. Her contribution is meta-level: the preconditions for value creation rather than value creation itself.
