The frame, stated correctly

The thesis's core claim is the manager as the central actor in value creation — but the impulse is external. Managers observe a changing environment they did not create, then steer and apply to turn what they sense into value. So this is externally triggered, managerially mediated — not "inside-out" (which would wrongly imply value invented from within).

Observe external signals→ Steer — reshape · leverage · navigate→ Apply — workflow · harness→ Value outcomes — managed portfolio

"Drive / mediate" are kept (they come from your Figure 1 caption and §4 intro) but are now always tied to these coded terms — e.g. "it is by observing, steering, and applying that managers drive and mediate value." If you'd still rather drop the words, that's a one-line swap (see plan).

Argument blueprint — key points & supporting literature

For each section: the points to land, and the specific claim from each paper that can build them. Tags: §2 developed in your literature review · NEW a lens leaned on but not yet in §2 (see the call-out after this section).

5.1.1

Continuation of digital transformation (kept)

Point: agentic AI value creation extends the DT pattern (technical + organizational conditions; efficiency as a first-order benefit) — but with three differences: governance flips from enabler to inhibitor, broader applicability yields skill-extension, and the faster pace makes managerial behaviour matter more.

Literature
  • Vidal et al. (2022) §2.4 — DT produces strategic + operational change from the opportunities/threats of digital tech; management's role (managing technical talent, prioritising learning, driving business change) is central. → transfer the digital-leader argument to the marketing manager.
  • Enholm et al. (2021/2022) §2.4 — link AI capabilities to competitive value via TOE enablers/inhibitors; efficiency a first-order effect; governance an enabler. → confirm conditions/efficiency; contradict on governance (we find it the chief inhibitor).
  • Holmström (2022) §2.4 — AI readiness = capabilities, data, governance, learning practices built before value appears. → confirm the readiness precondition.
  • Ancillai et al. (2023) §2.4 — the digital paradox: value mediated by complementary assets (skills, data, process, structure, leadership). → confirm; we give it micro-level content.
  • Gebauer et al. (2020) §2.4 — digitalization paradox; traps incl. failing to build partner trust. → extend: a far wider range of steerable elements than the named traps.
5.1.2

Extending research on AI in marketing (new)

Point: the field already frames AI value as a situated managerial process and calls for work beyond content generation; this study answers it three ways — (1) empirical content for "translation work", (2) shifts weight from the technology to the manager, (3) widens value beyond the efficiency/content lens to a managed portfolio.

Literature
  • Huang & Rust (2021) §2.2 — marketing sits at the data/tech/creativity/customer/commercial boundary; their framework is organised around types of AI and their task capabilities. → contrast: prior frameworks centre the technology; we centre the manager.
  • Vaid et al. (2025); Prasanna & Kushwaha (2025) §2.2 — AI contributes across the whole marketing process, not only communications. → confirm scope.
  • Enholm et al. (2022); Kitsios & Kamariotou (2021); Prasad Agrawal (2023) §2.2 — AI value requires alignment with strategy/knowledge/decision-making (not an isolated project) + "translation work"; TOE adoption factors. → confirm "situated managerial process"; we give it empirical content.
  • Mogaji & Jain (2024); Jain et al. (2024); Kim (2025) §2.2 — explicit calls for empirical research on GenAI / agentic AI in marketing. → this study is one of the first grounded answers.
  • Wahid et al. (2023); Grewal et al. (2025); Kumar et al. (2025) §2.1Kumar new — GenAI-marketing scholarship concentrates on content/creative work. → contrast: we move beyond content to the value-creation process.
5.1.3

Observing

Point: the manager is a sensor of external signals (AI progression, market pressure incl. agents-of-customers, supplier roadmaps); recognition is widely shared, so it is not the scarce capability — the discriminating act is the optics-versus-reality discipline (testing what is actually in production).

Literature
  • Teece (1997, 2007) NEW — dynamic capabilities; sensing = identifying opportunities and threats in a changing environment (a microfoundation). → frames "observing" as sensing. Lens not in §2 — see call-out.
  • Mogaji & Jain (2024); Kim (2025) §2.2 — consumers' changing behaviour / advertising in the age of agentic AI. → ground "agents of customers" as a sensed signal the literature already worries about.
  • Study's own: the optics-vs-reality gap and "agentic AI has become almost a marketing term" (§4.1.2, §4.3.1) — the empirical core; no external source needed.
5.1.4

Steering — the managerial repertoire

Point: managers act through a three-part repertoire (reshape / leverage / navigate) with a systematic navigation bias (own-team conditions reshaped; externally-originating ones — esp. governance — navigated, never reshaped); the AI champion is the pivotal actor; and value is won or lost here, in bringing people along and actually starting.

Literature
  • Teece (1997, 2007) NEW — seizing and transforming/reconfiguring microfoundations; asset orchestration (managerial coordination of co-specialised resources). → frames steering as seizing/reconfiguring; the champion gives it micro-content. Not in §2.
  • Cieslak & Valor (2025) §2.2 — employee resistance to change undermines AI readiness. → confirms resistance as the binding constraint; underwrites the navigation bias.
  • Weber et al. (2023) §2.2 — organizational capabilities for AI implementation (coping with inscrutability and data dependency). → supports capability-building as reshaping work.
  • Romeo & Lacko (2026) §2.2 — drivers and barriers of AI adoption. → situates the repertoire among known adoption forces.
  • Hanelt et al. (2021) NEW — DT demands more malleable organizational designs and continuous adaptation. → light support for reshaping; in reflist, not in §2.
5.1.5

Applying — from workflow to harness

Point: applying is a managerial design act — decompose the workflow, insert AI only where reliable, engineer the harness — and configuration is one component of value, complementary to steering, not its sole determinant.

Literature
  • Orlikowski (2000) NEW — practice lens: a technology's structures and effects are emergent in use, not embodied in the artifact ("technologies-in-practice"). → theoretical warrant for configuration-in-use. Not in §2.
  • Vargo & Lusch (2017) NEW — service-dominant logic: value is co-created; resources are operant; technology participates as an actor rather than a passive instrument. → frames the harness as a co-creating configuration. §2.5 gestures at co-creation but §2.3 doesn't develop it.
  • Kaartemo & Helkkula (2018); Leone et al. (2021) NEW — AI acts within, and enhances, value co-creation (SLR; B2B/healthcare case). → supports the agent-as-actor reading. Not in §2.
  • Enholm et al. (2022); Kitsios & Kamariotou (2021) §2.2 — the "translation work" of turning a possibility into a bounded, governed use case. → names the applying act in the literature's own terms.
5.1.6

Value outcomes — a managed portfolio

Point: value is a portfolio (benefits/sacrifices/risks produced together) the manager composes and benchmarks; created value ≠ captured value; Table 4 (comparable use cases, divergent outcomes) is the evidence of managerial mediation, externally triggered; and the negative terms are contained by governing AI with AI on a comparative error standard.

Literature
  • Woodside et al. (2008) §2.3 — value = "relative sum of total weighted benefits perceived − relative total costs perceived"; perception-based and multidimensional. → the formal backbone of the portfolio framing.
  • Almquist et al. (2016) §2.3 — 30 elements of value across functional ("saves time", "reduces effort"), emotional, and life-changing categories. → map the benefit types across operational/customer/strategic levels.
  • Doshi & Hauser (2024) §2.3 — GenAI raises individual creativity but lowers collective novelty (homogenization). → value destruction as a managed condition; the differentiation risk in §5.2.2.
  • Brynjolfsson et al. (2025) §2.2 — (specific) a ~14% productivity gain for support agents, largest for less-experienced workers. → grounds the efficiency + skill-extension benefit, and supports the junior-paradox inversion. Currently cited only for value-ambiguity — use the specific finding.
  • Lepak et al. (2007) NEW — value creation (use value) is distinct from value capture (exchange value), which depends on isolating mechanisms; multilevel. → would properly ground the "created ≠ captured" claim. In sources/, not in §2 or the reflist.
