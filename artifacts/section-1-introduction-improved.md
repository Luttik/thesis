Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI), a class of AI systems that produce novel text, images, audio, and code from patterns learned in large volumes of training data (Grewal et al., 2025), has rapidly become a central concern for marketing. Of all business functions, marketing and sales are projected to capture the largest share of GenAI's economic value, on the order of one-third of its total expected potential (Mayer et al., 2025). In practice, marketers already apply GenAI across a range of tasks: drafting and localizing campaign copy, generating product and creative imagery, synthesizing customer insight from unstructured feedback, personalizing messages at scale, and powering conversational customer-service assistants (Grewal et al., 2025; Wahid et al., 2023; Mogaji & Jain, 2024; Brinker & Riemersma, 2025). ***[ADD ONE COMPANY-SPECIFIC EXAMPLE HERE; see mapping note on comment 9/47]***

Yet across these applications, GenAI today functions largely as a question-and-answer machine. Its use clusters around brainstorming and content generation rather than the autonomous execution of the recurring tasks that fill a marketer's day (Brinker & Riemersma, 2025; GBK Collective & Wharton Human-AI Research, 2025). The technology answers; in most organizations it does not yet do the work.

A more recent and quickly evolving set of technologies is changing this. By giving large language models access to external tools and to the surrounding context in which a task unfolds, these advances allow AI to move from answering questions to acting on them (Acharya et al., 2025). The result is agentic AI, which Acharya et al. (2025) define as "autonomous AI systems that undertake to finish a set of complex tasks that span over long periods of time without human supervision" (p. 18913). Where GenAI generates content on request, agentic AI builds on the same generative models but adds autonomy, tool use, and goal-directed behaviour, enabling it to pursue objectives within a changing environment (Acharya et al., 2025). It is therefore far better suited to the execution of a marketer's ordinary daily work and, in principle, to creating value well beyond content generation.

This shift raises a question that is, as yet, largely unanswered. Marketing is at once the function with the most to gain from AI and the one where current use remains shallowest, concentrated on content (Mayer et al., 2025; GBK Collective & Wharton Human-AI Research, 2025); agentic AI is the development most likely to close that gap; and the pace of change leaves managers without established frameworks or empirical evidence to guide their decisions. The intersection of these three domains (marketing, agentic AI, and value creation) is precisely where understanding is thinnest. Although scholars have begun to map GenAI's possibilities in marketing (Wahid et al., 2023; Grewal et al., 2025; Abou Elgheit, 2025), the specific question of how marketing managers create value with agentic AI remains empirically unexplored, and researchers have issued explicit calls to address it (Kim, 2025; Mogaji & Jain, 2024; Jain et al., 2024).

This study therefore explores the question: How do marketing managers create value with agentic AI? The question is deliberately exploratory, reflecting a field that is still emerging and in which both theoretical understanding and practical guidance remain underdeveloped. To structure the inquiry, three sub-questions are addressed:

1. How do marketing managers identify and translate agentic AI opportunities into marketing applications?
2. How do marketing managers adopt agentic AI within their organizations?
3. What benefits, sacrifices, and risks does agentic AI create in marketing, and how do marketing managers manage them?

Focusing specifically on agentic AI, rather than on GenAI and its now-familiar content tasks, is a deliberate choice. Agentic systems are both more capable and more complex than the generative tools that preceded them: they act rather than merely respond, and that autonomy widens the range of value they might create while also introducing new conditions, dependencies, and risks. Examining this more demanding technology allows the study to look past the prevailing emphasis on content generation and toward the fuller spectrum of value (and difficulty) that agentic AI introduces into marketing work.

This focus addresses a clear gap. Despite intense attention to GenAI in both academic literature and practitioner discourse (Brinker & Riemersma, 2025; Mayer et al., 2025), little is understood about how marketing managers can move beyond content generation to create value systematically, and still less about how the autonomy of agentic systems reshapes that task. Existing accounts of digital transformation offer a useful starting point, since the rollout of AI can be read as a new wave of such transformation (Holmström, 2022); but they were not written for technologies that act with this degree of autonomy and evolve this quickly, and so cannot be assumed to transfer wholesale.

This study makes both theoretical and practical contributions. Theoretically, it offers one of the first grounded empirical accounts of how marketing managers create value with agentic AI, answering the calls for research noted above. Its central contribution is a model of managerial value creation that links observing a volatile environment, building the organizational conditions for change, steering the organization, designing how the technology is applied, and managing the portfolio of benefits, sacrifices, and risks that results. The account's organizing insight is that value is managerially mediated rather than technologically determined: comparable use cases produced markedly different outcomes across organizations, and the difference lay in the managerial work and configuration surrounding the technology rather than in the technology itself. The technology, in short, is necessary but far from sufficient. Practically, the study offers marketing managers a vocabulary and a sequence for this work, from recognizing opportunity, through building conditions and steering adoption, to composing and governing the resulting value, including the recurring pattern in which agentic AI is deployed to govern the very risks it creates.

The remainder of this thesis is structured as follows. Chapter 2 reviews the relevant literature and the calls for research within this domain. Chapter 3 sets out the methodology. Chapter 4 presents the findings. Chapter 5 discusses these findings in relation to the literature, develops their theoretical and practical implications, and considers the study's limitations and directions for future research. Chapter 6 concludes with the study's main contributions.
