Methodological note · chapter 4 (findings)

Does it make sense to add citations to Chapter 4?

Short answer: only sparingly, and only for specific kinds of citation. The Findings chapter in a constructivist grounded theory (CGT) study is a description of what participants did and said, anchored in the coded data. Comparison with literature, theoretical interpretation and contribution claims belong in the Discussion chapter.

Recommendation in one paragraph

Keep citations in Chapter 4 minimal and limited to methodological framing (e.g. Charmaz, 2014; Blumer, 1969) and to back-references to the Literature Review (e.g. “as set out in §2.1.4”) when a participant uses a technical term that needs grounding. Do not add citations that confirm, extend or contradict prior empirical findings — those belong in Chapter 5. In the current draft of thesis/4. Findings.md, the only citation present is the right one: (Charmaz, 2014) introducing the coding vocabulary.

1. The CGT argument

Constructivist grounded theory is explicit that the coding pass should stay close to the data; the researcher’s reading of the literature is treated as a sensitising input, not as a competing argument inside the analysis. Charmaz (2014, p. 117) frames this as a separation of voices:

“We use sensitizing concepts as points of departure to look at data, to listen to interviewees, and to think analytically about the data … without forcing prefabricated ideas onto our data.”

If the Findings chapter is dense with citations, two practical risks arise:

  • Voice confusion. The reader cannot tell which claims come from the participants and which come from the literature. The analytical contribution becomes harder to assess.
  • Double work. The Discussion chapter has nowhere left to go — comparison and interpretation have already been pre-empted. This blurs the line between Findings (what was seen) and Discussion (what it means).

Strict CGT writing therefore reserves comparative and interpretive citations for the Discussion chapter, and uses Chapter 4 to develop the analytical vocabulary that the Discussion will then put into conversation with the literature.

2. Where citation in Findings is still legitimate

That said, an MBA Findings chapter is read by examiners who expect to be able to anchor terms, methods and definitions on a single read. A small, disciplined set of citation moves is appropriate:

DoMethodological references

When you name the coding method, the sensitising concepts or the analytical vocabulary you are using, cite the methodological source.

Example: “…the analytical vocabulary that emerged from initial and focused coding (Charmaz, 2014)”.

DoBack-references to Chapter 2

When a term in the data risks being misread, cross-reference the Literature Review rather than adding new external citations.

Example: “…agentic AI (see §2.1.4 for the working definition)”.

DoDefining a term participants used

If a participant uses a technical term that has not been introduced anywhere else (e.g. MCP, RAG), a single short citation on first use is acceptable.

Example: “…new vendor-supplied connectors and protocols (MCP; Anthropic, 2024)”.

MaybeOne framing citation per major section

A single citation that names the lens you are using to read the data — e.g. value as a portfolio, dynamic capabilities — can be acceptable if it is purely organising. Prefer a sentence that points back to §2 instead.

3. Where citation in Findings is not appropriate

Don’tCompare your finding to an existing study

“This is consistent with Holmström (2022)…” belongs in Chapter 5. In Chapter 4 it pre-empts your own contribution.

Don’tInterpret a category through a theory

“Through the lens of dynamic capabilities (Teece et al., 1997)…” is a Discussion move. In Findings, stay descriptive.

Don’tUse a citation as evidence for a participant claim

If a finding is grounded in the coded data, the evidence is the quote, not an external reference. Adding a citation here actively weakens the chapter.

Don’tCite to legitimise terminology you coined

Codes such as full-stack performance or capability-translation process are analytic constructions of this study. They should not be bolstered with citations — they are the contribution.

2. First-use parentheticals for two technical terms. “Model Context Protocol (MCP; Anthropic, 2024)” and “retrieval-augmented chat (RAG; Lewis et al., 2020)” would help an examiner who reads Chapter 4 without re-reading Chapter 2. Single-citation, no interpretation, no commentary.

If you want either of these added to the current draft, I can do it as a small edit pass. They add four references at most, and they keep the chapter inside the strict CGT convention.

6. Quick checklist before submitting Chapter 4

  • Every citation in Chapter 4 is either methodological or a first-use definition.
  • No citation in Chapter 4 compares findings to other empirical studies.
  • No citation in Chapter 4 interprets a category through a theoretical lens.
  • Cross-references to Chapter 2 use section numbers (e.g. §2.1.4), not external citations.
  • All comparative, interpretive and theory-building citations are in Chapter 5.
Reference: Charmaz, K. (2014). Constructing grounded theory (2nd ed.). Sage. Specifically chs. 5–6 on coding and chs. 7–8 on memo-writing and theoretical sampling for the separation of analytical voice from literature engagement.
