## 4.2 Navigating the organizational context

Navigating the organizational context describes how managers steer or move around conditions within the organization. The organizational context can be divided into three key scopes: (1) the marketing department, which the manager can steer to create alignment with AI initiatives; (2) technical resources, which can be leveraged to implement AI initiatives, though interaction with external stakeholders is typically necessary to shape or allocate them; and (3) the governance framework, which bounds the potential for AI initiatives and which marketing managers do little to influence.

### 4.2.1 Steering the marketing department

The most consistently identified barrier to adopting agentic AI is not technical but organizational. As Interviewee 14 put it, “thirty percent of all AI trajectories is just technology … sixty to seventy percent is actually us as humans or as an organization.” The difficulty lies in the scale of the change that AI demands; Interviewee 12 described it as inherently hard:

> Change is frigging hard. It’s hard for us as individuals. Generally speaking, individuals don’t like to change, particularly if they’ve been doing something a certain way, and it works well, and they know it, and they’re comfortable. […] That challenge gets multiplied when you start talking about an organization … you’ve got multiple people, you’ve got politics, existing incentives, existing processes.

The manager’s task is to steer the department through this. As Interviewee 4 put it, “in leadership, you need to set goals. You need to inspire people to believe in what you are doing and pull the people together.” Steering the marketing department comprises three recurring behaviours: bringing people along, creating space for experimentation, and setting direction.

**Bringing people along.** The most prevalent steering behaviour is bringing people along: ensuring that employees are both willing and able to adopt AI. It works against resistance. Part of the response is making people feel valued through the change; Interviewee 4 noted that “people are drinking from a fire hose. And they need to have that leadership that tells them their importance, they’re part of the team, and their work matters.” Interviewee 13 connected this to the work itself: “Yes, we will automate these kinds of things, but that still means someone needs to look over this automation. You can learn to do this.”

Education and training are the principal mechanism. At its simplest this means making the value concrete; Interviewee 9 described why: “People don’t know it, so they just go back to doing their own thing until someone puts it right in front of them and says, this is what it is and this is how you use it in your day-to-day.” Peer-to-peer teaching proved effective. Interviewee 8 described “going through training sessions with my coworkers, showing them the agents that are being developed, or that they themselves can develop an agent … it becomes something that they can quickly go back to and work efficiently with.” Beyond ad-hoc training, several interviewees stressed that learning must be pitched to the level of the audience. Interviewee 10 described a three-level model:

> Help people learn what AI can do […] leadership, middle management, and employees, each with different focus. Leadership must understand where AI is heading to create strategic horizons. Middle management must learn how to facilitate and structure experimentation. Employees must learn that AI is more than writing emails.

**Systems and process thinking.** Education, however, reaches only so far. Beneath a simple lack of AI literacy sits a deeper competency that proved much harder to build: the ability to see one’s own work as a set of connected processes and to reason about which of them could be improved. Interviewee 10 put the gap most sharply: “in many marketing teams it’s very clear what the output is, while the way people get to that output is poorly refined … the marketing manager has no idea how people get to the output. So how on earth can you then decide what to automate?” He saw “very few places where marketing is treated as a set of processes,” warning that “if you don’t make that shift, everything keeps seeming difficult.” Interviewee 13 described the work of closing that gap: clients “want to understand their marketing data better,” yet “you have 20 different sources, you don’t know how to connect them,” so the real task is “analyzing what … processes you are doing right now that take time and that are not useful to do manually.” Part of the difficulty is cultural: Interviewee 1 observed that marketers “find perceived value in sitting on a complicated process” and must learn to “decouple the art from the science and use technology for what it’s worth.”

What gives these observations their edge is *who* made them. Systems and process thinking surfaced almost entirely in the accounts of the external advisors and technically grounded participants (Interviewees 1, 10, and 13); it did not appear as something the in-house marketing managers recognized about their own teams, and the data contain no example of a manager working to build it. This asymmetry should be read cautiously given the small number of in-house marketing managers in the sample, but it is suggestive and is revisited in the Discussion.

**Creating space for experimentation.** A second behaviour addresses the climate in which change happens, specifically whether the team can act under uncertainty rather than stall. The opposing failure mode is analysis paralysis. Interviewee 12 was direct: “You have to act. You can’t just be in a mode of studying this or writing memos about it.” Interviewee 4 observed the same cost: “We spend so much time debating which shot we have to take that that is already some cost.” The problem deepens where the culture punishes failure; Interviewee 7 described a setting in which a failed pilot is met with “Next time, you’re fired,” with the result that employees “will never start anything again.”

Managers respond by creating structured room to learn by doing. Interviewee 6 summarized the orientation: “I’d rather have something live and test and improve than be two years down the road and then finally have something live,” and Interviewee 15 echoed the bias toward action: “we’re more of the just-do-it type than mitigating all risks in advance.” Interviewee 12 described the conditions this requires: “the factory where the trains are running on time” alongside “the laboratory, where you’re in a position to experiment and try things and have things fail and break,” transferring an approach into the factory “only once you’ve found things in the laboratory that you’ve proven.”

This depends on leadership backing, and the two are reported together. Interviewee 13 described enabling it at the team level: “Provide freedom to experiment and don’t set this atmosphere of fear that AI is going to replace you.” Sustaining it requires endorsement from above, because, as Interviewee 12 noted, early learnings “don’t always provide immediate returns” and so must be deliberately supported. Interviewee 8 described how decisive that backing was: “She just let me go and run … the buy-in of my immediate management is pivotal to what I do.” Interviewee 6 likewise called “leadership encouragement … a tailwind,” noting that “whenever we use [agentic AI] and showcase something … it is appreciated and rewarded.” Backing can also unlock resources directly: Interviewee 8 recounted leadership deciding “this new AI group would eat the cost” so that “anyone within the corporation can ‘use it for free’.” Backing is the one element of steering that reaches beyond the team itself: managers do not control their own leadership, but they actively lobby it.

**Setting direction.** A third behaviour is setting direction: naming where the team is going with AI and giving that direction force. The first part is clarity: describing where the organization is heading, which use cases matter, and what success looks like. Interviewee 6 captured the posture: “This is the vision. This is where we’re going. Help me get there.” Interviewee 1 noted the cost of its absence: “If you don’t have clarity, jumping into execution will only surface a lot of problems.” Interviewee 10 located where clarity most often breaks down: “Middle management is often forgotten, but they receive strategic targets and must operationalize them. That’s where much of the friction sits.”

Direction is also carried by people, and is often embodied by an AI champion: the manager, or a technical employee backed by their superiors, who drives initiatives through personal initiative, visible results, and internal advocacy. Interviewee 9 described how disproportionately impactful such a person can be: an internal platform “created originally by … our director that dabbles in development … has blown up, and so now there’s millions of dollars of funding.” Interviewee 8, who fits the description, captured the working style: “There’s a new thing out, it takes me five seconds to test it, and then I know if it’s ready for something that we might want to deploy. And at that point, I try to get buy-in.” Champions typically combined technological know-how with business understanding, which let them produce the examples that earn direction and senior support.

**Cross-cutting tensions.** Three tensions cut across these behaviours without belonging to any single one. The first is a talent-pipeline paradox: agentic AI is deployed first on the easiest, usually junior, tasks, which Interviewee 10 warned erodes development (“then you no longer develop juniors into seniors … no new talent pipeline”), even as juniors prove the fastest adopters, inverting the usual seniority of expertise into what Interviewee 7 called “the pyramid on its top.” The data offered no resolution. The second is a gap between personal and organizational effectiveness: agentic AI is, individually, genuinely easy to start with, as Interviewee 3 noted, “it makes life so much easier”; yet an unspoken barrier separates that personal ease from effectiveness at organizational scale. The third is a gap between optics and reality, in which capability is claimed before it exists; Interviewee 9 described the dynamic candidly: organizations “say that you have something that you don’t really have, and then you go and build it really quickly,” which Interviewee 11 attributed to hype that must be managed down: “AI is being overpromised all the time. And we have to reduce expectations.”

The department, then, is where the manager has the most room to act, but that room has a limit. When the team’s own capacity is exhausted, managers look beyond it: the subject of the next scope.

### 4.2.2 Leveraging technical resources

The second scope concerns the technical resources on which implementation depends: data, tooling, infrastructure, and the technical talent to connect them. Some of these resources sit inside the marketing department and some do not, and that distinction shapes how much the manager can do about them: when conditions are favourable they are leveraged, but the data show no clear pattern of marketing managers reshaping them. Interviewee 6 captured the resulting relationship: the CMO should be “the CTO’s best friend,” because the CTO’s cooperation is necessary to execute her vision.

Data and infrastructure emerged as the foundational prerequisite, and for agentic AI the stakes run higher than for generative tools alone: unless the data and tools are accessible within the organization’s systems, agentic systems cannot act on or interpret them, which limits what they can do at all. Interviewee 10 made the boundary concrete: “technology strongly defines what can and cannot be done in marketing. Your MarTech stack bounds what personalization you can do.” The team’s own collected data and marketing-technology stack are, broadly, theirs to organize, but the deeper layer typically belongs elsewhere. Interviewee 2 attributed his organization’s slow progress to exactly this, noting that “the infrastructure is not yet in place, and the workload is really big,” while Interviewee 10 lamented “how badly we still handle data.” A counterpoint came from Interviewee 13, who argued that getting started need not wait for elaborate infrastructure: one can “just tell the agent, ‘I have data in this source, this source, and that source. Please look at it,’” after which “this agent grabs all the data from different tools.” And where the data is accessible, the gains are concrete. Interviewee 4 connected an agent straight to his analytics stack: “I connected an MCP to their Google Analytics and Google Search Console … and have an agent run reports.” The result was “much better reporting” for “a workflow that was not there” before, replacing the manual routine of having to “download from two different system[s], then in Excel … cross correlate,” work that not long ago required “a giant analytics team.”

The availability of technical talent emerged as a constraint of its own: the people who can build, implement, integrate, and operate agentic systems. Several interviewees described simply not having them. Interviewee 6 was direct: “we don’t have the skills yet to actually do it.” Interviewee 2 described an organization with “a lot of people” overall but “not a lot of people working on the digital landscape.” Interviewee 13 argued that this gap, more than the technology itself, is where initiatives fail: “you buy gen AI, you buy a big tool, you have no one taking care of that,” a pattern he summarized as “unoperationalized tool effectiveness.” Interviewee 5 captured how the bottleneck has shifted: where the technology “used to always be very cumbersome … the most expensive part,” the limiting factor is now “having the right people … available.”

Where the skills are present, the effect is the opposite. Interviewee 3 described a team that, being “more digital,” was correspondingly “more adventurous,” wanting to “grow in the digital domain,” while Interviewee 14 drew the line between dabbling and competence: “people are experimenting and quite a lot is happening … but if you want to use AI well, you still need skills.” Some traced their progress directly to that foundation: Interviewee 11 put his organization’s smooth adoption down to prior investment, noting that “we have been invested in our data operations for a long time” and that “a lot of the data people already did a lot of deep learning … [which] helped us a lot.” And where the technical talent sits with the manager it compounds: Interviewee 10 had “built a kind of operating system for our own company: CRM, to-do’s, everything in one setup,” something he “could only imagine because [he] had already built many smaller things.”

Where this internal capability is unavailable or saturated, the most common response is to source it externally. The motivation is speed. Interviewee 6 was blunt about the alternative: “using experts externally, because if we would’ve waited for people internally … it would’ve been a major blocker,” the internal route meaning “I’m going to wait two years before I get space on the backlog.” Interviewee 13 described the same move where internal teams were overwhelmed: “we are now really building a big clean room with another company because the internal IT team is completely overwhelmed.” Yet the interviewees were clear that the bypass carries a cost. Interviewee 13 warned that while “agencies give you a kickstart,” the real danger is that “too many companies make themselves dependent on external workforce,” leaving “no one internally who understands how this is working”; the knowledge ends up held by freelancers rather than the organization. Routing around the bottleneck buys speed now at the risk of dependence and stalled capability later.

### 4.2.3 Dealing with governance

The third scope concerns the governance framework: the compliance restrictions, restrictive processes, and organizational politics that bound what may be attempted. Here the manager’s room to act is smallest: marketing managers largely accept the framework as given, and no attempt to alter or negotiate governance policy was observed. Interviewee 13 observed a recurring pattern in large enterprises, in which the very category of agentic AI is treated as out of bounds even where comparable tools are not: “It’s totally fine to buy any kind of tool you don’t know anything about and put [data] into the system. But agentic AI? That’s completely out of the question.” Governance can also constrain adoption indirectly by limiting the collection or use of the data on which it depends; Interviewee 13 voiced the concern, “are we allowed to track data from our customers?” It can also dampen the culture: Interviewee 10 described how “first everyone does bring-your-own-AI; then someone says ‘this is maybe not such a good idea’ and suddenly everything is banned. Then the most enthusiastic people either disengage or leave.” Interviewee 6 noted that such structures, while legitimate, can prevent organizations from learning at all.

Rather than change the framework, managers work around it. Interviewee 15 was candid about the personal route: “If your company says it’s not allowed, well then I would still do it privately … not with your company’s data or insights, but think up your own things and go experiment with it.” Interviewee 13 noted that “shadow IT can be a very good lighthouse project to make people understand what is possible.” However, while this lets employees deploy agentic AI outside the governance framework, it was not put forward as a sensible management strategy.
