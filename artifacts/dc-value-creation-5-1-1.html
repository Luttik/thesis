Where does the chain end? Dynamic capabilities, reconfiguration, and value creation

Your question: are dynamic capabilities even involved with value creation, or does it end with the reconfiguration of resources? The short answer is that the theory does not stop at reconfiguration — value is the very thing it exists to explain — but the value sits downstream of reconfiguration and is only loosely tied to it. That loose coupling is the gap your thesis fills.

In one line

Reconfiguration renews what a firm can do; value appears only when the renewed resources are orchestrated in use, and is kept only under further conditions of appropriability. Your data says the binding constraint lives in that orchestration step — the step dynamic-capabilities theory names but leaves empirically thin.

1 · What the theory is actually a theory of

It is tempting to read dynamic capabilities (DC) as "the ability to reconfigure resources," full stop. But Teece, Pisano & Shuen (1997) introduced the construct to answer a value question: how do firms achieve and sustain competitive advantage in fast-changing environments? Reconfiguration is the mechanism; advantage — i.e., value — is the explanandum, the thing the whole apparatus exists to account for. So value is not appended after the theory; it is the theory's reason for existing. The DC are upstream means to it.

In the standard microfoundational reading, the apparatus has three clusters — sensing, seizing, and transforming (reconfiguring). Crucially, value capture is folded into seizing from the start (business-model design, investment discipline, capturing returns). The theory therefore reaches all the way to value on its own terms — reconfiguration is one cluster of three, not the finish line.

2 · The chain, drawn out

The clearest way to see your question is to lay the causal chain end to end. The three DC clusters renew the resource base; a bridge step deploys that base in practice; and only then do the two value outcomes appear. Your model's own categories map onto it (blue italics).

3 · Three reasons reconfiguration isn't the finish line

a. A renewed resource base still has to be used

Transforming produces a renewed set of resources; it does not, by itself, produce output. The actual value-producing work is done by ordinary (operational) capabilities deploying that base day to day. DC change the toolkit; value comes from orchestrating the toolkit on a real problem. This is exactly your applying category — and it is why "reconfigured" and "value-creating" are not the same state.

b. Having the capability does not guarantee the value

The DC are necessary but not sufficient. Two firms can hold comparable capabilities yet land very different outcomes, because the link to performance runs indirectly through the resource configuration each one actually builds, and through differences in timing, cost, and learning. This is the precise theoretical home of your Table 4 result: nominally identical use cases, divergent value, explained by configuration rather than capability. (In the literature this is the Eisenhardt & Martin / Zott line — see the citation card.)

c. Value created is not value captured

Even once value is created, the firm keeps it only under the right appropriability conditions — complementary assets, isolating mechanisms, the absence of easy imitation. Your §2.4 already separates creation from capture; Lepak, Smith & Taylor (2007) is the reference that formalises it. Your model speaks mainly to creation; capture (can a configuration-based advantage resist imitation?) is the natural next problem it raises.

4 · The bridge has a name: asset orchestration

The step between "renewed resource base" and "value created" is not a void in the theory — Teece calls it asset orchestration: the managerial work of combining and co-aligning resources so they actually generate returns. The trouble is that the construct stays abstract; the literature rarely specifies what the orchestration consists of in a given setting. That is the opening your study walks through. Your harness (data access, memory, tools, guardrails, prompt, model choice) plus the surrounding conditions and managerial behaviours are a concrete, microfoundational account of asset orchestration in the agentic-AI setting. Framed this way, you are not merely relocating a constraint inside sensing→reconfiguring — you are completing a link the theory leaves underdeveloped.

5 · So — to your question, directly

  • Dynamic capabilities are absolutely involved with value creation; the framework was built to explain value, not just resource change.
  • But it does not end at reconfiguration. Reconfiguration renews the base; value is created one step later, when the base is orchestrated in use, and captured one step later still.
  • That orchestration step is real in the theory but thin in the evidence — which is precisely why your grounded account of the harness and conditions is a contribution rather than a restatement.

5.1.1 Extending dynamic capabilities to agentic AI in marketing

The model extends dynamic capabilities theory (Teece et al., 1997) into the specific context of agentic AI in marketing, and in doing so locates the study's contribution at a particular point in that theory's causal chain. Dynamic capabilities describe a firm's capacity to integrate, build, and reconfigure its resource base in response to a changing environment; the theory's purpose is to explain how firms create and sustain advantage under that change, so value is not external to the framework but the outcome it exists to account for. The model's elements map onto this apparatus. Observing corresponds closely to digital sensing (Ellström et al., 2021; Warner & Wäger, 2019). Steering — reshaping, leveraging, and navigating organizational conditions — performs the seizing and reconfiguring work through which the resource base is renewed, building the literacy, data access, clarity, and protected space a capable agent requires. Applying, however, is analytically distinct from this renewal: it is the deployment and orchestration of the renewed resources in use — the configuration through which an agent actually acts. Separating these two moments matters, because it is the second, not the first, that the data identifies as decisive.

This separation exposes where the binding constraint sits, and it is not where the theory's emphasis would predict. Sensing was not the scarce capability: participants across roles and organizations observed the same hockey-stick progression (Section 4.1.1), the same competitive and consumer-driven market pressure (Section 4.1.2), and the same vendor roadmaps (Section 4.1.3). Nor was the renewal of the resource base sufficient on its own; comparable investments produced divergent outcomes for nominally identical use cases (Section 4.4.4; Table 4). What separated organizations was the orchestration of resources in use — the configuration in which a renewed resource base was actually brought to bear on a marketing problem. Dynamic capabilities theory anticipates this step in the abstract, through the notion of asset orchestration (Teece, 2007) and the long-standing argument that the value of a digital technology is mediated by complementary assets rather than realized automatically (Ancillai et al., 2023; Enholm et al., 2022; Holmström, 2022), but it has remained empirically thin about what that orchestration consists of. The contribution of this study is to give that step concrete, microfoundational content in the agentic-AI setting: it specifies the configurational work — the harness, the conditions, and the managerial behaviors around the agent — through which a renewed resource base is converted into realized value.

Read this way, the study does more than relocate the locus of differentiation from opportunity recognition to organizational follow-through; it completes a link the theory leaves underdeveloped. Reconfiguration renews what a firm can do, but value is created only when those resources are orchestrated in use, and captured only under the further conditions of appropriability and complementarity that govern who retains the value once created (Lepak et al., 2007). The model speaks principally to creation: it accounts for how marketing managers turn a widely available technology into value through the configuration they build around it, and it leaves the durability of that value — whether a configuration-based advantage can resist imitation — as the natural next problem the framework raises.

Glossary — hover a dotted term
Sensing
Identifying and shaping opportunities and threats in the environment. Your observing category.
Seizing
Mobilising and committing resources to an opportunity, including business-model and investment choices. Already contains value capture.
Transforming / reconfiguring
Renewing the resource base — recombining, adding, retiring resources. The cluster your question asks about.
Ordinary capabilities
The operational "doing" capabilities that run the business day to day. They produce output; DC change what output is possible.
Asset orchestration
The managerial act of combining and co-aligning resources so they actually yield returns — the bridge from a renewed base to value.
Appropriability
How well a firm can prevent others from capturing the value it creates (imitation, bargaining, spillovers).
Complementary assets
The surrounding resources (data, skills, channels, process) without which a technology's value is not realised or kept.
