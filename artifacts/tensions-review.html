Findings chapter · cross-cutting tensions review

Which tensions belong where?

This document evaluates the sixteen candidate cross-cutting tensions that surfaced in the coded interview material and in the analytical memos. For each tension I propose a placement — Findings, Discussion, Both, or Drop — and give the rationale. The intent is to make explicit which patterns are descriptive (and should sit in §4 of the thesis) and which are interpretive claims that should be held back for §5.

Findings (§4) Both (short in §4, longer in §5) Discussion (§5) Drop / future research
Decision rule used. If the pattern can be stated as what participants did or said, it belongs in Findings. If it requires interpretation against literature or makes a strategic / theoretical claim, it belongs in Discussion. Tensions that recur across blocks and are anchored in memos but are still descriptive go in §4.7; tensions that only make sense as commentary go in §5.

2. Tension-by-tension evaluation

01

Hype vs reality — agentic, RAG, workflow or just chat?

Participants describe ambitious "agentic" use cases that, on close reading, are mostly retrieval-augmented chat, scripted workflows or simple automations. Even AI-fluent participants struggle to draw the line between agentic AI, AI agents, workflows of agents, RAG and classical ML.

Anchors: memo Hype v.s. reality; Hard time classifying AI (notes.md); code Agentic has: vague definition (Scott Brinker); Erica Hahn (Ruby chatbot — "Is it agentic? Yes. I think that is agentic. I would still doubt if it’s all agentic…").
"People call things agents that you could debate and say, 'I don’t know, is that really an agent? How agentic is it?' … it’s become almost a marketing term."
Scott Brinker
BothFindings: name the pattern
Discussion: theoretical implication for the definition of agentic AI
02

AI hygiene vs AI leadership — followers and leaders

Most adoption is "doing the proven thing efficiently" (chatbots, content drafts, analytics) — what the memo calls AI hygiene. Only a handful of participants describe genuinely differentiating moves (Erica's multi-vendor chatbot bet, Dennis's full Obsidian/Claude stack, Tim's vibe-coded marketing infrastructure).

Anchors: memo Getting to outsized returns; Unsustainable arbitrage opportunity; codes Tailwind > Experimenting with AI, Internal development, Following vendors' AI strategy.
DiscussionThis is an interpretive synthesis (strategy claim), not a code-level pattern.
03

Friction and time-lag from potential to adoption

A clear, recurring observation: technical possibility runs ahead of the tools, the tools run ahead of what organisations adopt, and what organisations adopt runs ahead of what individual employees actually use. Tool calling has existed since late 2023; MCPs and Skills exist now; but very few users leverage them.

Anchors: memo Friction with AI; Time to insights; codes Obstacle > Lacking understanding of AI, Obstacle > Lacking systems thinking or process knowledge, Gap between optics and reality.
FindingsDescriptive, cross-block — anchor of §4.7.
04

Full-stack performance, not just data

Quality of AI depends on the entire stack — data plus master prompt plus tools plus planning plus memory plus model choice. Only Erik talked explicitly about using a harness to improve AI quality; most participants under-recognise this technical layering.

Anchors: memo AI performance; codes Tailwind > Leveraging: systems or process thinking, Leveraging a solid harnass / context engineering; Erik Hilhorst, Tim Wiegel, Dennis Goedegebuure.
BothFindings: describe the stack participants build.
Discussion: theoretical contribution — value depends on a configured socio-technical stack.
05

AI champions are disproportionately powerful

A handful of individual champions consistently push initiatives forward (Erica at Basic-Fit) or build something that gets picked up and scaled by leadership (Omnicom). Without a champion, momentum stalls.

Anchors: memo AI champions; Clear messaging; codes Tailwind > Backed by leadership, Tailwind > Bringing people along (change management); Erica Hahn, Lauren Stokowski.
FindingsWithin §4.4.3 Leading and governing AI work.
06

The optics gap — what is shown vs what is in production

Multiple participants describe a gap between the AI demos that are pitched (or held up internally) and what is actually running in production. Sylvia's BDR-email experiment is the canonical example — customers see through it quickly.

Anchors: memo Looking good / optics; code Gap between optics and reality (15 applications · 8 docs); Sylvia Vroklage, Lauren Stokowski.
FindingsWithin §4.4.2 Building capacity to change.
07

Time to insights speeds up the learning loop

Agentic AI compresses the time between question and insight, which compresses the broader sense → seize → reconfigure loop. Participants describe this as a learning-velocity effect rather than a raw productivity effect.

Anchors: memo Agentic AI reduces time to insight; codes Benefit > increases speed, Benefit > increases depth/quality of insights; Tim Wiegel, Maarten Mantjes, Berfun Goodwin.
FindingsWithin §4.6.2 Quality, insight and decision value.
08

Efficiency is not a sustainable advantage

If everyone uses the same models for the same use cases, efficiency gains converge. Scott names this directly; Lauren observes that her agency's approach is "much in line with competitors."

Anchors: memo Unsustainable arbitrage opportunity; Scott Brinker (universal benefits only); Lauren Stokowski.
DiscussionStrategic claim — needs to be argued against the dynamic capabilities and digital-transformation literature.
09

Build vs buy — and the agency-shaped middle

Participants make varied sourcing choices: pure build (Tim's vibe-coded data pipelines), pure buy (Salesforce, HubSpot, Emarsys, Braze), multi-vendor (Erica's chatbot stack), or vendor-following (Floris). Agencies sit in the middle — clients look to them for AI capability but agencies struggle to allocate their own AI budget.

Anchors: memo Build v.s. Buy; Looking to Agencies; codes Adoption mode and sourcing (42), Internal development (13), Multiple vendors / solutions (7), Following vendors' AI strategy (4).
FindingsWithin §4.5.2 Adoption mode and sourcing.
10

Upfront costs vs ROI — budgets stay tough even in AI-forward orgs

Even when ROI is reported at 10× (Erica's chatbot programme), getting the upfront budget is hard. There is a structural tension between the speed at which AI value can be realised and the speed at which budgeting cycles can be unlocked.

Anchors: memo Upfront costs & ROI; codes Benefit > 10x ROI on chatbots, Drawback > Cost; Erica Hahn, Lauren Stokowski.
FindingsWithin §4.4.3 Leading and governing AI work + §4.6.4 Sacrifices.
11

"AI sells" — selling AI vs selling outcomes

Multiple participants observe that pitches and proposals win partly on the involvement of AI rather than on demonstrated outcomes. Depending on the organisation, AI is either promoted or actively discouraged in client-facing materials.

Anchors: memo Looking good / optics; codes Tailwind > The ability to sell AI, Market, client or vendor pressure; Lauren Stokowski, Maarten Mantjes.
FindingsWithin §4.3 External input.
12

Hallucinations + vibe-coding paradox

Participants name hallucination as a real risk and even warn against using AI outside one's domain of expertise (Scott), yet everyone is "vibe coding" — building production-adjacent things in domains where they cannot fully validate the output.

Anchors: memo Hallucinations and Code; Building with AI; codes Risk > Hallucinating (11), Usecase > building/prototyping solutions/products with AI; Scott Brinker, Tim Wiegel, Erik Hilhorst.
DiscussionA genuine paradox; needs interpretation against risk-management and capability literature.
13

Hybrid insights — qualitative + quantitative in one loop

Because AI can process both qualitative and quantitative data, participants describe combining market/desk research with their own measured data to get insights they couldn't get before.

Anchors: memo Hybrid insights; codes Usecase > ANALYSING DATA / creating insights, Benefit > increases depth/quality of insights; Tim Wiegel, Andreea Bulisache.
FindingsWithin §4.6.2 Quality, insight and decision value.
14

Embracing human imperfection as a brand counter-move

Floris suggests marketers should deliberately leave human mistakes in content so audiences can tell it is human. This is a strategic counter-move to AI-driven homogeneity but currently appears in only one interview.

Anchors: code Embrace human mistakes (in expression); Floris Reguoin.
DiscussionSingle-source, but theoretically rich; raise in Discussion as a brand-distinctiveness point alongside Doshi & Hauser (2024).
15

GEO — generative engine optimisation

Participants mention GEO as a concern (their brand showing up in LLM answers) but nobody describes a working solution.

Anchors: memo GEO; notes.md.
DropUnder-evidenced for §4. Flag in Discussion's "future research" section.
16

Agent-to-agent (A2A) communication and the coexistence of agents and humans

Floris and Andreea hint at a future state where agents talk to other agents (including adversarial extraction attempts) and where organisational structure has to reckon with "coexistence of agents and humans." Currently speculative.

Anchors: codes Agent-to-agent communication, AI transformation will probably lead to a new reorg and coexistence of agents and humans; Floris Reguoin, Andreea Bulisache.
DropFuture-state speculation; not part of the current value-creation process. Flag in Discussion's "future research" section.
