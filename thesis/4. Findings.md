# 4. Findings

This chapter presents what marketing managers and AI experts described doing with agentic AI. It stays close to the coded interview data and uses the analytical vocabulary that emerged from initial and focused coding (Charmaz, 2014). The interpretation against the literature is held back for Chapter 5.

The chapter is organised around a working model that emerged through constant comparison of the initial and focused codes. A moving **AI progression** sets a technological backdrop; organisations are pulled into action by **external input** from clients, vendors, markets and regulation; they respond by mobilising three sets of **internal conditions** — available resources, capacity to change, and leadership and governance — which together shape **AI adoption** (use cases, sourcing modes, and the human-AI operating model). Adoption in turn produces **value outcomes**, understood as a portfolio of benefits, sacrifices and risks rather than a single outcome variable. Section 4.7 then steps back and names four cross-cutting tensions that recur across these blocks.

## 4.1 Reading guide

The empirical material consists of seventeen semi-structured interviews with marketing managers, internal AI champions and external AI advisors (see Appendix B for the pseudonymisation key and role profile). Quotes are reproduced from the coded QDA-XML project with speech disfluencies preserved; mid-quote elisions are marked with an ellipsis (…), and interviewer turns are preserved with `[Me]` markers and participant turns with `[Them]` markers where they appear in the original transcript. Counts cited below are *code-application counts* in the coded corpus and are not used as claims about statistical prevalence.

Two design choices shape what follows. First, the heaviest empirical load sits in the *internal conditions* block (395 code applications across 17 documents), with *value outcomes* (330 / 17) and *AI adoption* (270 / 17) close behind. *External input* is comparatively thin (27 / 10), and *AI progression* even thinner (11 / 6). This distribution itself is a finding: participants describe value creation primarily as an inside-out process. Second, the chapter prefers gerund-phrase labels — *bringing people along*, *building capacity to change*, *mobilising available resources* — to keep the analytical vocabulary close to action.

Throughout the chapter, participants are referred to by pseudonym (P1 to P16). The mapping between pseudonyms and roles is summarised in Appendix B; pseudonyms are blind to organisational identity.

## 4.2 AI progression as a moving backdrop

Participants treat agentic AI not as a stable product but as a fast-moving capability shift. The dominant gerund is *keeping up*, and most participants describe falling behind. P14 framed the pace with a hockey-stick metaphor that captured the way new tools displace each other within weeks:

> Because these developments are going so fast, they really go in a, in a hockey stick. Um, yes, ultimately, right — what was spectacular all at once within Gemini tomorrow is, uh, taken over all at once by Claude the day after, and the week after that we're, uh, two steps further with ChatGPT. … Yes, you get a Swiss army knife at your disposal with 100,000 more functionalities than you could ever have conceived on your own knife.
>
> — *P14*

The pace is widely felt but rarely tracked formally. P8 — who explicitly maintains an AI experimentation practice — described having given up on systematic model comparison:

> What I originally used to do was test every single model that would come out. You know, the, the, the iteration was slow enough that it could be done. Now I c- I c- I can't even keep up. … Are we on five two or five three for ChatGPT? I don't even know.
>
> — *P8*

A second feature of the progression is the *blurring of agentic and "AI"*. Even AI-fluent participants struggle to draw the line between agentic AI, AI agents, workflows of agents, retrieval-augmented chat, and classical machine learning. P12 named this directly:

> People call things agents that you could debate and say, "I don't know, is that really an agent? How agentic is it?" You know, how much autonomy, how much decision-making? It, it's, it's become almost a marketing term, uh, more so than a technical definition.
>
> — *P12*

P13 made the same point from a builder's vantage point, noting that an "agentic" workflow is often functionally equivalent to a sufficiently well-written prompt and that the conceptual distinction does not always do work in practice ("in the end, every agentic tool is a very good prompt, very well describing what the process should be"). The empirical implication is that the *category* of agentic AI is unstable in participants' accounts; what is stable is the experience of a continuously broadening *Swiss army knife* of capabilities that pulls organisations forward whether or not they have settled on a definition.

## 4.3 External input: clients, vendors, markets and regulation

Although AI progression is felt at the level of the technology, the *pressure to act* arrives through external channels — clients asking for AI in their proposals, vendors shipping new connectors and standards, markets shifting and regulators tightening. External input is, however, a comparatively thin empirical block (27 applications, 10 documents). Participants describe being pulled by outside actors more than pushed by them.

The clearest pull comes from the *ability to sell AI*. P9, working in a large agency network, described how internal positioning was forced into the open by client demand:

> I wouldn't say the first instance, but the biggest instance where we had to really understand, like, what is really, like, our end-to-end agentic offering? 'Cause you have different agencies in Omnicom sometimes offering the same thing. Like, for example, CGI pipeline capabilities. So you have our production company. They have their own tools. We have our, our digital agency within the precision marketing vertical has their own tools. So it was a really interesting experience of, like, identifying who owns this capability really.
>
> — *P9*

The same client pressure can also reshape the deliverable itself. P10 observed that clients now understand AI-augmented work more easily if it is packaged as software than as services, even when the underlying work is identical:

> What we see now is that people understand it better when you deliver a software solution. It starts as a marketing problem, then it's a process problem, and the deliverable becomes software. I find that inconvenient, but I sell it more easily that way.
>
> — *P10*

Vendor activity is a second channel. Several participants described new vendor-supplied connectors and protocols (MCP, Skills) as the proximate trigger for new use cases. P15 illustrated this with a webshop connector for Claude that turned the company's own product catalogue into a re-order channel:

> Look, you can couple connectors to Claude. … We've built a connector for one of our webshops, um, that you can for example couple to Claude and tell Claude: okay, fetch all my orders from \[supplier]. … And then via that connector it actually reorders that order, you throw it into my shopping cart. … I see that as the future, so to speak. It's a new channel, we need to facilitate that.
>
> — *P15*

Regulatory and market constraints are felt unevenly. P13, working with German enterprises, repeatedly described GDPR not so much as a rule set but as an enforced caution that pre-empts experimentation: "this whole thing is laid out in many companies in a way that they are making themselves less competitive." Where the field structure itself constrains adoption — for example, when innovation work is institutionally cordoned off from the live data platform — participants experience external constraints as internal ones. P2 described this institutional cordon at a national sports federation:

> We see with the major project that we worked on last two years and also the next two years, it's centralized with the of course, the data, platform and with the new initiatives around it … so that we have a — yeah, it's connected to our own data platform and to and there, and from there on we can better, yeah, make use of agentic solutions because the structure of data and everything is better then.
>
> — *P2*

Overall, external input matters more as a *legitimising prompt* than as a designing force. Clients, vendors and markets give marketing managers permission to act, and sometimes a specific use case to act on, but they rarely supply the resources, the capabilities or the governance arrangements that adoption requires. Those have to be assembled internally.

## 4.4 Mobilising internal conditions

Internal conditions are the densest empirical block in the data (395 code applications across all 17 documents). Participants describe three reinforcing sets of conditions that determine whether external pull translates into adoption: the *resources* they can bring to bear, their *capacity to change*, and the *leadership and governance* they operate under.

### 4.4.1 Mobilising available resources

Across interviews, three resource constraints recurred: the availability and accessibility of *data*, the *skills* needed to act on it, and a broader *capability gap* around systems thinking and process knowledge.

Data availability is the most frequently named precondition. P13 captured the way data backlogs neutralise nominally AI-capable tools:

> If I look at my customers, there's now tools that have AI use cases like Emarsys, Braze and so on, but people still don't use this because there's no time to put the right data in from the data warehouse. There's like a long list of backlog, and there's no one in the team itself that understands how to really bring their marketing knowledge and this IT data knowledge together.
>
> — *P13*

Where data is available, participants describe a step-change in what AI can do. P4 illustrated this with a personal stack — Obsidian as a "human interface" on top of Claude Code, with files synchronised across GitHub and per-client vaults — that turned scattered context into operational memory:

> CoWork can help me building out the, my workflows and operationalize all the things that I do- To get more efficiency out of myself. … I have persistent memory using, um, Obsidian. And I just… My productivity just went 10X. And this is a thing, um, that we need to drill down in, you know, on your, on, for your project because this is where marketing also is going.
>
> — *P4*

The problem of *which* data to collect is itself part of the resource constraint. P5 described a hard case from the consumer side: a B2C organisation in which the relevant customer attributes do not exist in any system because customers have little reason to declare them.

> So collecting that data is very difficult because why, as a customer, would I ever give it to you? And it changes rapidly because some people are just using a camper for two years, and then they're done with it, or a lot of things change.
>
> — *P5*

The second resource constraint is *skills*. Skill gaps are described both at the technical end (no one on the team can wire up tools, write queries, or evaluate model output) and at the marketing end (no one can specify what good output looks like). P13 named the failure mode that follows from buying tools faster than the team can absorb them:

> You buy gen AI, you buy a big tool, you have no one taking care of that. … It can also be something like you buy a tool like Funnel, you buy a tool like Adjust or like Tableau because this is now the thing you need, but there's no one understanding what the tool does and no one responsible for the tool. … I mean, summing this up, I think the biggest failure is unoperationalized tool effectiveness.
>
> — *P13*

A third and more analytical resource constraint runs through several interviews: a *capability gap* in systems thinking and process knowledge. P13 again articulated this most directly, returning to it as a structural pattern across his client base:

> It's, on a high level, it's analyzing what are the processes you are doing right now that take time and that are not useful to do manually. … But really the biggest thing is to sit together with them and really try to understand what these processes are.
>
> — *P13*

This gap matters because it shapes what counts as a viable use case. Participants who do well with agentic AI tend to be those who can decompose a workflow into its data, decision and action layers; those who do less well describe their work as "we need AI", which P13 repeatedly characterised as starting from the wrong end.

### 4.4.2 Building capacity to change

The second condition is the organisation's *capacity to change*. Three patterns recurred: explicit *resistance*, an *experimentation posture* that varies sharply between organisations, and a *gap between optics and reality* in what is actually being deployed.

Resistance is the densest single code in the obstacle family. It is rarely cast as opposition to AI specifically; instead, it is described as a more general loss-aversion. P13 — coining a phrase from a German colleague — used it as a recurring analytical handle:

> "Germans are not risk averse. Germans are loss averse." And I think there's also this like, "Oh, if it's not automated, what, what is my team doing?" So that's like, it's always stupid discussions about this.
>
> — *P13*

The same pattern appears in participants from very different organisations. The fear is not that AI will fail; it is that successful AI will reveal previously unmeasured discretionary work. As a result, resistance often shows up downstream as governance friction (see §4.4.3) rather than as direct objection.

The mirror image of resistance is *experimentation*. The capacity to run small, fast, repeated experiments emerges as the most reliable bridge between technological possibility and actual adoption. P13 described a "lighthouse-project" pattern designed to make experimentation legible to legal and IT:

> I typically try to do y- lighthouse projects, right? … Don't do anything, uh, with all the data at the company. Don't put it to the whole IT, security, legal stuff. Just go to legal, say, "Hey, we have this data set, and with this kind of data, we want to do something." … And usually before that, I need to get the buy-in from the stakeholder, from the people in the company, but they usually get me because they want to build something like this.
>
> — *P13*

P5 described a similar pattern at a small consumer organisation. Once an in-house route-generation system had proved itself internally, the team simply expanded the same vibe-coded approach to a prototype app, with the explicit understanding that they would seek external help only if they outgrew their own capacity. P14, working from a vendor and consultancy perspective, framed experimentation more broadly as the marketing skill of the moment — knowing how to brainstorm *with* and *against* a prediction model rather than only within it; this experimentation argument is revisited in §4.6.2 in connection with quality and creativity.

Across interviews, a third strand of change capacity is the *gap between optics and reality*. Participants repeatedly described demonstrations, pitches and internal narratives in which AI was present, while in production the same workflow remained manual, scripted, or partially automated. P15 gave the canonical example, in which automated BDR emails were quickly seen through by customers:

> There was also an experiment done with generating emails from a BDR to customers. Well, the customer sees through that pretty quickly: nice that you still want to be personal and now you're sending me this, so, just a bit too much of a shortcut with this use case. So no, but I think that's where the challenge is. … But then I think: okay, but what has really come to execution? So it has really helped you enormously. And how has it subsequently come to execution? That's where the challenge lies.
>
> — *P15*

This optics-reality gap matters analytically because it suggests that what participants *call* agentic AI in their organisations is often not the same as what is running. The gap is closed not by adding more technology but by sustained operationalisation — which is the work of the third internal condition.

### 4.4.3 Leading and governing AI work

The third internal condition is *leadership and governance*. Four recurring patterns shape it: the role of *AI champions*, the need to assemble a whole *operational model* rather than a single capability, the work of *bringing people along*, and the friction generated by *high bureaucracy around AI*.

Several interviews describe the disproportionate impact of individual *AI champions* — people whose job description may not include AI but who push initiatives forward by lobbying, experimenting and absorbing political cost. P6 functions explicitly as such a champion in her own organisation, repeatedly described as the person who keeps pulling the chatbot, personalisation and journey-orchestration use cases forward when momentum slows. P9 plays a comparable role inside a global agency network, where she had to coordinate a cross-agency mapping of who actually owns each agentic capability (quoted in §4.3).

The second pattern is the recognition that AI value depends on assembling a whole *operational model* rather than fixing a single component. P9 articulated this in a key passage that recurs across her interview:

> It's like a pie. It's like you have to have all these pieces in order for it to be successful. It can't just be successful because the guy at the top building it built this really cool product. The, the people have to understand it, they have to know, they have to feel good about engaging with it, selling it. It has to be governed properly, it has to be priced properly. Yeah, I, I don't think there's one thing. I think that it is a whole operational model that needs to be built around it in order to be successful.
>
> — *P9*

A third pattern is *bringing people along*. Where leadership invests in the communicative, training and change-management work that surrounds an AI rollout, participants describe much stronger adoption than the technology alone would predict. P13 described the conditions under which loss-averse teams accept automation:

> For Germans, or at least for most of the Germans I, I, I work with on these kind of things, it's feeling secure that this doesn't impact their livelihood. … If the C level is there and helps you and tells you, "Hey, yes, we will automate these kind of things, but that still means someone needs to look over this automation. You can learn to do this." And someone being a change manager, in the worst case, managing people out in a nice way, I think this is really something that, uh, is very, very important.
>
> — *P13*

The fourth pattern — *high bureaucracy around AI* — is described as a near-mirror image of the first three. Even in AI-forward organisations, participants reported committee-driven approval processes that delayed access to off-the-shelf models for many months. P13 again gave the most concrete example:

> One of my customers where it took me one year to make them understand that they need Funnel.io just for grabbing the marketing data, right? … And at this customer, even when we made it, it took, uh, three different committees to go through. … So it needed to go through the IT committee, to the cloud committee, to the AI committee.
>
> — *P13*

A related budgeting tension recurs across cases. Even when participants reported very strong ROI on agentic AI investments — P6 estimated tenfold returns on a chatbot programme — *upfront* budgets remained hard to unlock. The result is that the speed at which AI value can be realised consistently outruns the speed at which budgeting cycles can be unlocked. Leadership and governance therefore work as either an accelerant or a brake on the rest of the internal conditions: the same data, the same skills and the same change capacity can produce very different adoption outcomes depending on whether the surrounding governance is treated as enabling or as a compliance check.

## 4.5 Translating possibility into practice: AI adoption

AI adoption appears in the data as three layered choices: which *use cases* to pursue, what *sourcing mode* to use, and what *human-AI operating model* to wrap around them.

### 4.5.1 Use case archetypes

Four use-case archetypes emerged most strongly across the coded material: *analysing data and creating insights* (39 applications, 15 documents), *creating and validating content and campaigns* (28 / 12), *automating manual processes* (21 / 12), and *general-purpose personal assistants* (15 / 9). Two further use-case families — *building and prototyping solutions with AI* (10 / 5) and *customer-facing agents* — appear less often but feature prominently in specific cases.

The analytics archetype is the most general. P13 described an agentic marketing-mix-modelling workflow in which the agent pulled data across sources, reasoned about its quality, and proposed the next analytical step:

> There's pretty much now an agent where you can just dump your data. The agent looks at the da- not, not even dump your data. You just tell the agent, "I have data from this source, this source, and this source. Please look at this. These are the accounts. Then I have two CSV files which give you my out of home data, and now please check whether this makes sense to use for marketing mix modeling." And then this agent actually, well, grabs all the data from the different tools and, uh, gives you information about, "Hey, this is good. You need to do more about this, more about this. The data quality there doesn't fit."
>
> — *P13*

The content archetype is the most visible to outside observers but, by participants' own accounts, the least transformative. The dominant use is augmenting existing copywriting and creative workflows rather than redesigning them. P5 illustrated this in a small consumer organisation that had effectively rebuilt its route-content library with generative AI but had been careful not to delegate the underlying route design.

The *automating manual processes* archetype is where the term "agentic" begins to do real work in participants' accounts. P13 described his own steering-committee preparation as an agentic chain in which a transcription tool, Claude and a Notion page collaborate to draft the next agenda:

> What I usually have is, uh, a transcribing tool which then Claude goes to and, for example, I have steering committees every two weeks. That means on a Monday afternoon, Claude goes into my t-transcription tools, gets all the different, uh, transcriptions of meetings for this one customer and starts writing down what I should talk about in the steering committee.
>
> — *P13*

The *general-purpose personal assistant* archetype is closely related and overlaps with the previous one. Most participants who described agentic AI as transformative for their own work were describing this archetype. The *customer-facing agent* archetype is less universal but is described as commercially decisive where it is used. P12 emphasised the qualitative shift in customer-service agents over the past two years:

> The most common example is things like the customer service, uh, you know, sales assistant agents on websites that actually, I mean, they've kind of been around for years, but they largely sucked. Um, you know, but over this past year or two, uh, they've actually gotten really good because they're leveraging the conversational capabilities of these LLMs.
>
> — *P12*

P6 — running such an agent in a consumer membership organisation — described it as both a customer-experience asset and a commercial channel, where the same agentic capability that handles routine queries can also recognise an upgrade moment and convert it.

### 4.5.2 Adoption mode and sourcing

Across cases, four sourcing modes appeared: *internal development*, *off-the-shelf vendor adoption*, *multi-vendor stacks*, and *following the AI strategy of an incumbent vendor*. The choice is rarely a single decision and is usually layered: organisations buy where there is an obvious product, build where the off-the-shelf solution does not fit, and follow when a strategically important vendor releases AI features.

P13 made the case for *building internally* — but with the explicit caveat that internal teams often build behind the market. He described data teams spending months recreating capabilities that already existed in OpenAI or Claude. The internal-development mode is therefore both a sign of capability and, at times, a sign of strategic drift.

P6 described a deliberately *multi-vendor* sourcing mode for chatbots, where multiple architectures coexist precisely to support experimentation and to allow targeting of specific needs. The trade-off — additional cost and integration overhead — is accepted because the organisation has a baseline to measure against (a pre-LLM chatbot programme) and because the upside of getting it right is substantial.

A third mode — *following an incumbent vendor's AI strategy* — appears in cases where an organisation has standardised heavily on a single platform and treats that platform's AI roadmap as a default. P4 described pinning his entire personal stack to Claude on the basis that the surrounding ecosystem was moving fastest in that direction. This mode is efficient when the chosen vendor is a leader but creates lock-in risk if the platform falls behind.

A fourth and structurally distinctive mode is the *agency-as-AI-channel*. Marketing agencies sit in an awkward middle: clients increasingly look to them for agentic capability, but agencies struggle to allocate their own AI budget because the business model rewards billable hours rather than software investment. P9 captured this tension in her account of building an end-to-end agentic offering across an agency network.

### 4.5.3 The human-AI operating model

The third adoption choice is the *human-AI operating model* — the division of work between human marketers and agentic systems. Three patterns recurred: *human in the loop* on output, *human in the loop* on judgement, and *coexistence* arrangements where agents and humans share workflows.

The *human-in-the-loop* pattern is present in nearly every interview. Even participants who described aggressive automation were careful to preserve a check on output before it reached a customer. P5 described this explicitly for route content:

> So, so far we've only produced routes with AI that have been actually driven by someone. Uh, a camper enthusiast, like, uh, uh, someone who's a volunteer at our organization, for instance. … So it's all AI driven, but the core sort of routes is human generated.
>
> — *P5*

The judgement variant of human-in-the-loop is more demanding. Several participants — particularly those running analytical use cases — described agentic AI as producing work that is "as good as a mediocre analyst" but requiring sense-checking by someone who could recognise a wrong answer:

> You can always build something very quickly that gives you the same information as if you would have an analyst sitting there or some kind of a controller or whatever, because the same work is done in there. It's not as good as if you would have a great analyst or great controller, but it's pretty much the same as if you have a mediocre one and, uh, still have to sense check what comes out of it.
>
> — *P13*

The most ambitious operating-model accounts came from participants who described the next step as a redesign of the organisation itself. P1 captured this in the language of coexistence:

> Um-… this transformation will probably lead into redesigning the org structure, and then see what serves you. You start thinking about the coexistence of agents and humans.
>
> — *P1*

P12 systematised the operating-model question by distinguishing three kinds of agent: *agents for marketers* (tools that help marketers with their own work, such as planning and analysis), *agents for customers* (marketer-controlled agents that interact with customers, such as service or sales-assistant agents on websites), and *agents of customers* (consumer-owned agents acting on customers' behalf, for example a personal AI doing deep research or making an agentic purchase). P12 considered the third category the most disruptive for marketing because it dissolves the firm's traditional control over the customer interface. Most organisations in the data are operating predominantly in the first category, a handful in the second, and none yet in the third.

## 4.6 Value outcomes: benefits, sacrifices and risks

Value outcomes are reported in the data as a portfolio of effects rather than a single benefit. Four families emerged: *efficiency and performance gains*, *quality, insight and decision value*, *customer and relational value*, and a counter-family of *sacrifices, risks and value erosion*. Participants rarely separate these cleanly, and several of the strongest accounts contain all four families in a single passage.

### 4.6.1 Efficiency and performance gains

The dominant benefit category is efficiency. *Increases efficiency* (42 applications), *increases speed* (25), and *scales beyond humans* (16) together make this the most heavily coded value family. Participants do not usually translate efficiency into a single number; instead, they describe time saved that is reallocated to higher-leverage work. P13 gave the most representative formulation:

> Even if it's nothing where you're like, "Oh, I can now, uh, get rid of all my analysts," but you can at least enable the analysts to be 20% faster. And 20% doesn't sound much, and maybe doesn't even reflect properly in your numbers, but if they're 20% faster, I'm quite sure, … just this little bit being faster enables me to be of higher quality in all the other things I can't increase in speed, right?
>
> — *P13*

P4 was the most aggressive on the magnitude of the effect, describing a 10× productivity gain that he attributed to the combination of model access, persistent memory and a personal operating layer rather than to any single tool.

P6 reported the most explicit ROI figure in the corpus. Asked directly about the return on her organisation's chatbot programme, she answered:

> Yeah, I would say 10X. Let's say 10X. Because it's not two and it's also not 200.
>
> — *P6*

Across cases, efficiency gains are described with relatively high confidence; what is much harder, as discussed in §4.6.4, is to translate them into firm-level performance measures.

### 4.6.2 Quality, insight and decision value

The second value family is *quality, insight and decision value* — value that comes from doing the work better rather than faster. Three patterns recurred: agentic AI *accessing the entire database as context*, *helping users go beyond their personal skillset*, and *increasing the depth and quality of insights*.

P6 framed the context-access argument vividly when comparing her in-house chatbot with the outsourced human service it had partly replaced:

> I already dare to claim that the quality is much higher, than even if an agent does it, because we have all of our agents outsourced in Madagascar. They're amazing, they're fantastic people, but they are not always as aware as a database, obviously. Like, they don't have the whole database in their head.
>
> — *P6*

The *go beyond personal skillset* pattern is the most frequently named quality benefit. Participants describe agentic AI letting them work outside their formal training or seniority — code where they cannot code, analyse where they cannot analyse, write where they would normally hire a writer. P14 framed this in creative terms:

> If you know how to work smartly with AI, then you're more creative than anyone else. … You should not work within the predictions. You should work outside the predictions. … Then you come up with ideas that perhaps nobody had ever thought of before.
>
> — *P14*

P10 described the same effect in operational terms. With agentic AI, he could plug an analyst-equivalent into almost any decision point in his marketing operation:

> I keep returning to processes, but because we're constrained by capabilities and capacity. That game barely exists anymore in some areas. I can plug in a data analyst almost anywhere to support what I'm doing.
>
> — *P10*

A third pattern — *hybrid insights* — combines qualitative and quantitative information in a single analytical loop. Several participants described this as something they could not previously do at all without a multi-week handover between research and analytics. The implication is that the value of AI here is not only an improvement on an existing analytical workflow but a new one: insights that could not be produced at all by an existing team in an existing time budget.

### 4.6.3 Customer and relational value

The third value family is more heterogeneous and less heavily coded, but it is the one most directly visible to end customers. Two patterns recurred: *better personalisation* and *ease of use*. P6 described both in the same chatbot context, where the agent's reach into the customer record allowed for a more personal response *and* removed friction from membership management. P14 raised an opposite-pole concern: that the same personalisation engine, applied across many firms with similar models and prompts, may converge on indistinguishable output, prompting marketers to *deliberately leave human mistakes in content* as a brand counter-move. This concern was raised in only one interview but is theoretically pointed; it is picked up again in Chapter 5.

### 4.6.4 Sacrifices, risks and value erosion

The counter-family — *drawbacks, risks and value erosion* — appears in every interview. The three most heavily coded risk categories are *brand and output risk* (31 applications), *security, privacy and compliance risk* (19) and *drawback / sacrifice* more broadly (15). Within brand and output risk, the most frequent code is *hallucination* (11 applications across 7 documents).

P15's BDR-email example (quoted in §4.4.2) sits at the intersection of all three. The agentic system produced output, the output reached a customer, the customer registered the gap between the marketed warmth and the automated execution, and the brand experienced reputational sacrifice that was not visible in the team's own efficiency metrics.

Security and compliance risks are less dramatic but more pervasive. P13 described a recurring pattern in which the same organisation that was nervous about a single new SaaS tool was simultaneously running unsanctioned consumer-grade AI tools on the desktops of its marketing team:

> Data security-wise, uh, which is a big thing for Germans, uh, very problematic, but it happens everywhere.
>
> — *P13*

A second strand of value erosion is harder to measure: the slow homogenisation of marketing output that follows from many organisations using similar models, prompts and patterns. P14 raised this as the basis for his "embrace human mistakes" intuition, suggesting that perfectly polished AI output may itself become a negative signal.

A third, more reflective sacrifice is the *capability replacement risk*. Participants who described agentic AI as accelerating their own work also noted that they were no longer practising the underlying skill — analysis, writing, prototyping — at the same rate. This was not framed as a present-day problem but as a deferred one.

## 4.7 Cross-cutting tensions

Stepping back from the block-by-block account, four tensions recur across the coded material and across the analytical memos. They are summarised here in descriptive form; the interpretive treatment is reserved for Chapter 5.

The first is the *hype-versus-reality* tension. Many use cases described as "agentic" turn out, on close reading, to be retrieval-augmented chat or workflows with conditional logic. P12 was explicit that "agentic" has become "almost a marketing term"; P13 routinely noted in his own building work that he could not tell the difference between an agentic workflow and a very good prompt. The descriptive finding is that participants' category of agentic AI is unstable and frequently inflated, both internally and externally.

The second is *friction and the time-lag* between technical potential, the tools that embody it, what organisations adopt, and what individual employees actually use. The technical capability of tool-calling has been broadly available since late 2023; only a fraction of participants describe their teams using it routinely. The standards layer (MCP, Skills) is moving fast, but the adoption layer continues to lag. This pattern is visible across organisation types and sizes.

The third tension is *full-stack performance, not just data*. Participants who reported the strongest results — P4, P13, P5 — described an assembled stack of data, master prompt, tools, planning, memory and model choice, with explicit attention to how the components interact. Participants who reported underwhelming results tended to describe single-component fixes ("we got the data right", "we got the model right"). The descriptive finding is that the *configuration of the stack* explains more variance in reported outcomes than any single component.

The fourth tension is *upfront cost versus realised ROI*. Even where ROI is reported as very high (P6's 10× chatbot estimate is the clearest case), upfront budgeting remains a structural drag. The result is that AI-forward organisations and AI-laggard organisations differ less in their understanding of the benefit than in their willingness to release capital ahead of certainty.

Two further patterns appear in the data and the memos but are reserved for interpretation in Chapter 5: the distinction between *AI hygiene* (doing the proven thing efficiently) and *AI leadership* (building a differentiated capability), and the *hallucination/vibe-coding paradox* in which participants name hallucination as a risk while simultaneously building production-adjacent artefacts in domains where they cannot fully validate the output.

## 4.8 A working answer to the research question

Pulling the threads together, the data suggest a descriptive answer to the research question that is closer to a *capability-translation process* than to a discrete adoption decision. Marketing managers create value with agentic AI by *iteratively translating a moving technological backdrop into bounded use cases, conditional on the data, skills, change capacity and governance they can mobilise, while trading off efficiency and quality gains against brand, security and adoption risks.*

The empirical weight is on the internal side of this process. External pull legitimises action, but the determinants of whether action produces value are the resources, the change capacity, and the leadership and governance assembled inside the organisation. Use cases cluster into a small number of archetypes — analytics, content, automation, personal assistants and customer-facing agents — but their value depends less on which archetype is chosen than on how completely the surrounding stack is configured around it. Value outcomes are best described as a portfolio in which efficiency and insight gains coexist with brand, security and adoption risks, and in which the strongest accounts treat the trade-offs explicitly rather than treating value as a single quantity.

Chapter 5 takes these descriptive findings and reads them against the dynamic capabilities, value co-creation and AI-readiness literatures, in order to draw out where the data extend, qualify or contradict the existing accounts of value creation with generative and agentic AI.
